The first thing to understand about hand-lettering is that it is not fancy
handwriting. Handwriting is one continuous, fast motion. **Hand-lettering is
drawing letters** — slow, deliberate, built up stroke by stroke, often redrawn.
Once that clicks, the whole craft gets less intimidating: you are not being
judged on your natural penmanship, you are drawing shapes you can practise like
any other.

Here is how to begin.

## The tools are cheaper than you think

Ignore the expensive starter kits for now. You need almost nothing:

- **A pencil and a decent eraser.** Most lettering starts in pencil and gets
  inked later. The pencil stage is where all the real decisions happen.
- **Grid or dot paper.** Guidelines keep your letters consistent. Printer paper
  with lines drawn in works fine.
- **One brush pen** (a Tombow Fudenosuke or any firm-tip brush pen) for the
  classic thick-and-thin look, and **one fine-liner** (0.3–0.5mm) for monoline
  and outlines.

That is it. Add tools later when a specific project needs them, not before.

## Learn the one rule that makes lettering look "right"

Brush lettering has a single governing rule that beginners always miss:

> **Thick on the down, thin on the up.**

When your pen travels downward, you press hard and the stroke goes thick. When it
travels upward, you lift to a light touch and the stroke goes thin. That
contrast between heavy downstrokes and hairline upstrokes is what makes brush
lettering look like brush lettering. Break the rule and it reads as messy
handwriting; follow it and even simple letters look intentional.

Spend your first sessions on this alone: pages of downstrokes (thick), pages of
upstrokes (thin), then the basic connective shapes.

## Drill the seven basic strokes

Every lowercase letter in a script alphabet is built from a small set of
foundational strokes. Master these and the alphabet assembles itself:

1. **Entry stroke** — a thin upstroke that leads into a letter.
2. **Underturn** — down thick, curve, up thin (the base of *u*, *i*, *t*).
3. **Overturn** — up thin, curve, down thick (the top of *n*, *m*).
4. **Compound curve** — overturn into underturn (the body of *w*, *v*).
5. **Oval** — the round body of *a*, *o*, *d*, *g*.
6. **Ascending loop** — the tall loop of *l*, *h*, *b*.
7. **Descending loop** — the low loop of *y*, *g*, *j*.

Fill a page with each, slowly. This feels like handwriting homework because it
is — but it is the difference between guessing at letters and knowing how they
are constructed.

## Slow down more than feels natural

The most common beginner mistake, after ignoring thick-and-thin, is going too
fast. Speed is for handwriting. Lettering is closer to sketching: pause between
strokes, lift the pen, reset, and place the next stroke deliberately.

Watch any lettering artist and you will notice how much they stop. A single word
might take a minute. That deliberate pace is what gives you control over the
weight and shape of every line. If your letters look shaky, the fix is almost
always to slow down, not to buy a better pen.

## Faux calligraphy: get the look with any pen

You do not need a brush pen to make thick-and-thin letters. **Faux calligraphy**
gives you the same result with an ordinary fine-liner or ballpoint:

1. Write your word in normal cursive with a monoline pen.
2. Find every downstroke.
3. Draw a second line beside each downstroke to widen it.
4. Fill the gap in solid.

The result is indistinguishable at a glance from brush work, and it teaches you
exactly where the weight goes. It is also the most reliable way to letter on
surfaces where a brush pen will not behave — chalkboards, cards, wood.

## Your first real project: a one-word card

Do not start with a full quote. Pick **one word** — *hello*, *thanks*, a name —
and make a small card:

- Rough the word in pencil, lightly, on grid paper. Fix the spacing and the
  slant.
- Redraw it until the proportions feel even. Redrawing is not cheating; it is
  the job.
- Ink it with faux calligraphy or a brush pen.
- Add a simple flourish or a small drawn element only *after* the word looks
  right on its own.

One clean word beats a whole sentence of wobbly ones, and finishing a small
piece teaches you more than abandoning an ambitious one.

## Practise the same word for a week

Here is the drill that builds real skill: pick one short word and letter it once
a day for a week, on the same size paper, in the same style. By day seven you
will have solved the spacing, the connections, and the weight — and you will see,
laid side by side, exactly how much consistent practice buys you.

Keep those seven versions. Like a daily sketchbook, they are the honest record
that the slow, deliberate method works. Then pick a new word and go again.
