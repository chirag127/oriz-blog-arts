The single best thing you can do for your drawing is not a class, a tablet, or a
better pen. It is showing up tomorrow, and the day after. A daily sketch habit
turns drawing from a rare event you have to psych yourself up for into a plain
part of the day, like brushing your teeth. Skill follows attendance.

Here is how to build one that actually lasts.

## Shrink the habit until it is embarrassing

Most sketch habits die because the first version is too big. "Draw for an hour
every day" is a resolution, not a habit. You will miss a day, feel behind, and
quit by the end of week two.

Start with a commitment so small it feels silly: **one drawing, five minutes, on
one small page.** A single mug. A shoe. Your other hand. The rule is not "draw
well" — it is "make a mark." On a bad day, five honest minutes still counts. On a
good day you will naturally run over, and that is a bonus, not the target.

The point of shrinking the habit is to protect the streak. A streak you never
break is worth more than any single brilliant page.

## Choose a sketchbook you are willing to ruin

Buy a cheap, small sketchbook — A5 or pocket-sized, plain or lightly toned
paper. Expensive paper creates pressure to make every page a masterpiece, and
that pressure is exactly what kills a daily practice.

You want a book you can fill with bad drawings without guilt. Ninety percent of
what goes in a sketchbook is practice, not product. Number the pages if you
like, but do not curate. The mess is the record of your progress.

Keep one pen you trust — a fine-liner or a cheap fountain pen — clipped to the
book so the whole kit is one object you can grab on the way out the door.

## Anchor it to something you already do

New habits stick when they ride on top of an existing one. This is called
habit stacking: after I do X, I will draw for five minutes.

- After I pour my morning coffee, I sketch whatever is on the table.
- After I sit down on the evening train, I draw the person across from me.
- After I finish dinner, I draw the empty plate.

The existing routine is the reminder. You are not relying on motivation or a
phone alert — you are attaching the new thing to a cue that already fires every
day without fail.

## Draw from life, badly, on purpose

Copying photos has its place, but drawing from life — real objects in real
light — teaches you to *see*, which is the actual skill under drawing. Your
early sketches from life will look worse than your photo copies. That is
correct and temporary.

A useful drill: pick one object and give yourself two minutes. Do not lift your
eyes to check the page more than a few times; keep looking at the object. The
goal is not a good drawing — it is training your hand to follow your eye. Do
this daily and within a month you will notice edges, proportion, and negative
space you were blind to before.

## Kill the inner critic with volume

The fastest way past "I can't draw" is to draw so much that any single page stops
mattering. When page 40 is just one of hundreds, you stop treating it as a
verdict on your worth and start treating it as a rep.

Ceramicist teachers tell a version of this story: one group graded on the single
best pot, another on sheer weight of pots produced. The volume group made the
best pots — because they spent the term actually making, learning from each
failure, while the other group sat theorising about perfection. Drawing works the
same way. Quantity is how you buy quality.

## What to do when you miss a day

You will miss a day. The habit is not "never miss" — it is "never miss twice."
One skipped day is an accident. Two is the start of a new habit of not drawing.

When you slip, do not restart the counter in your head or punish yourself with a
huge make-up session. Just do your embarrassingly small five minutes the next
day. The streak that matters is the one measured in months, and it easily
survives the odd gap.

## A four-week starter plan

- **Week 1** — five minutes, one everyday object, from life. Any medium.
- **Week 2** — same, but add one two-minute "eyes on the subject" gesture drawing.
- **Week 3** — draw one thing that scares you: a hand, a face, a bike. Badly. Daily.
- **Week 4** — flip back through the book. Circle three drawings that surprised you.

That flip-through at the end of week four is the real payoff. You will see a
month of small, consistent effort stacked up as visible progress — proof that
the boring method works. Then you start month two.

Keep the book small, keep the sessions short, and keep showing up. The drawing
takes care of itself.
