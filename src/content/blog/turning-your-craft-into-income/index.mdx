At some point most makers wonder whether the thing they love could also pay for
itself. It can — but the version of "selling my art" that people imagine (open a
shop, list some products, wait for money) almost never works. Turning a craft
into income is its own skill, separate from the craft itself, and it rewards the
same patient, iterative practice.

Here is an honest map of the routes and the first steps.

## First, separate the goal from the fantasy

"Make a living from my art" is a huge leap. Most people who get there passed
through smaller, concrete goals first:

- **Cover the cost of the hobby.** Sell enough to pay for your materials. This
  alone changes how the work feels.
- **A regular side income.** A predictable few hundred a month.
- **Replace part of a day job.** Only realistic after the first two are steady.

Pick the nearest goal and aim only at that. Skipping straight to "quit my job" is
how people burn out and conclude, wrongly, that their craft "doesn't sell."

## Know the routes — they are not all the same work

There is no single "sell your craft" path. The main routes demand very different
skills:

- **Physical products** — you make and ship objects. High effort per sale, but
  the most direct. Markets, craft fairs, and your own small online shop.
- **Commissions** — bespoke work for a client (a pet portrait, a wedding sign, a
  logo). Higher prices, but you trade creative freedom for a brief and a deadline.
- **Digital products** — sell a file once, sell it many times. Printable art,
  lettering practice sheets, pattern packs, brush sets. Low effort per sale after
  the first, but you compete on discovery.
- **Teaching** — workshops, a class, a short course. Often the most reliable
  income for skilled makers, because people will pay to learn a craft they admire.
- **Content and audience** — building a following that later supports products,
  commissions, or sponsorship. Slowest to pay, compounding over time.

Most successful makers eventually blend two or three of these. But start with
**one**. Pick the route that fits the time you have and the work you actually
enjoy making.

## Make things people want, not only things you love

This is the uncomfortable part. The market does not pay for effort or for how
much you love a piece; it pays for things people want to own or give.

You do not have to sell out. You have to *listen*. Notice which of your pieces
get the most "I'd buy that" comments. Notice what people ask you to make. That
overlap — what you enjoy making **and** what others want — is your product.
Everything outside it is a hobby, which is fine, but it is not the income line.

## Price for profit, not for approval

Underpricing is the most common and most fatal mistake. New sellers price to feel
"reasonable," forget half their costs, and quietly lose money on every sale.

A workable starting formula:

**Price = (materials + your time at a real hourly rate + overheads) × a margin,**
then sanity-check against the market.

- **Materials** — everything the piece consumes, including waste and packaging.
- **Your time** — pay yourself a real wage. "Free" time is not free.
- **Overheads** — fees, shipping supplies, tools, the cost of the stall or listing.
- **Margin** — the multiplier that lets you restock, discount occasionally, and
  eventually grow. Doubling the base cost is a common floor, not a ceiling.

If the honest price feels too high for your market, the answer is usually to make
something with a better cost-to-value ratio — not to work for nothing.

## Start small and validate before you commit

Do not order 500 units or build an elaborate website first. Test cheaply:

1. Make a small batch of your best candidate product.
2. Sell it somewhere with built-in footfall — a local market, a community fair,
   an existing online marketplace.
3. Watch what sells, what people pick up and put down, and what they ask for.
4. Use that to decide your next, slightly bigger batch.

Real sales to strangers tell you more in one weekend than months of guessing.
Every batch is a rep; you are iterating your product the way you iterate a drawing.

## Treat the boring half as part of the craft

Selling comes with admin: photographing work well, writing clear descriptions,
tracking costs, packing orders, replying to messages, and — depending on where
you live — registering for tax once you earn past a threshold. Check your local
rules early; it is far easier than untangling it later.

None of this is glamorous, and all of it is the job. Makers who treat the
business side as a craft to be learned — with the same patience they gave their
first wobbly sketches — are the ones still selling in five years.

## The first three steps

If you do nothing else after reading this:

1. **Pick one route** and one product you both enjoy making and think people want.
2. **Price one piece properly** using the formula above, including your own time.
3. **Sell a small test batch** somewhere with existing traffic, and listen hard
   to what happens.

Keep it small, keep it honest, and let the feedback shape the next batch. That is
the whole method — the same one that built your craft, pointed at earning from it.
